Quizata

Why Quizata exists

Most of us carry a rough mental model of the world — how rich, how healthy, how fast-changing it is — built from headlines, not data. Those headlines skew toward what's alarming or novel, so the model drifts. Quizata is a two-minute daily check against reality: five real numbers, one slider each, no multiple choice to hide behind.

It's the same idea behind Hans Rosling's "Ignorance Test," turned into a daily habit instead of a one-time quiz.

Sourcing standard

Every statistic links to a real, reputable, primary source — the World Bank, UN agencies, IEA, Our World in Data, Pew Research, national statistical offices, and similar — along with the year the figure is "as of." Nothing is generated, estimated, or paraphrased from a secondary article. If we can't verify a number against its original source, it doesn't ship.

We also try to avoid stats chosen purely to shock — the goal is a more accurate picture of the world, not a scarier or rosier one.

Scoring

Each guess starts with an accuracy score, 0–100, based on how far it is from the real answer as a share of the slider's range (measured on a logarithmic scale for numbers that span orders of magnitude, like populations or dollar amounts, so "off by 2x" always costs about the same regardless of size). The score drops faster the further off you are: missing by 5% of the slider still earns about 84, 10% about 69, 20% about 44, and being 60% of the slider away or more earns 0. Leaving the slider untouched won't get you far — it takes a real estimate to score well.

Half your final score for a question is that accuracy, and half is how close you were to the crowd's average accuracy that day — so the game is measuring your sense of the world both against reality and against the crowd's. Being a little behind the average costs a little; only a genuinely large gap costs a lot. Early in a question's life, before enough people have answered, you're scored on accuracy alone. Your daily total is out of 500.

Once enough people have answered a question, the reveal screen also shows a third marker: the crowd's average guess, alongside yours and the real answer — often the more interesting comparison, since it shows whether the crowd as a whole leaned toward a common misconception or landed close to reality.

Power-ups

Once a day you can narrow a question's slider range to a third of its width, centered wherever you've currently got the slider — the real answer is always guaranteed to stay somewhere inside the narrowed window, so a rough instinct gets you finer control, and even a bad one just means the window shifts to somewhere you weren't looking rather than locking you out of a good score entirely. Once a day you can also take a second guess on any question, with the better of the two counting. Using either caps that question's maximum possible score, so they're a real tradeoff, not a free upgrade.

Your data

Your streak and history live only in your browser. What gets shared is minimal and anonymous: a running count, score total, and guess-value total per question per day, used only to compute the crowd comparisons above — never a per-player record of who guessed what.
